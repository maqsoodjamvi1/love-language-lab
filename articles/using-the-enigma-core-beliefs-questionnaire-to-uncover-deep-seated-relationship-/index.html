Have you ever found yourself in the middle of a minor disagreement with your partner, only to react with a level of intensity that completely shocks you? Maybe your partner forgets to text back, and suddenly you are convinced they are pulling away, leading you to send a barrage of anxious follow-up messages. Or maybe a casual piece of feedback at breakfast makes you shut down and withdraw for the entire day.

When our emotional reactions feel disproportionate to the actual situation, it usually means we have bumped into a deep-seated core belief. These are the rigid, unspoken rules we carry about ourselves, our partners, and how relationships work. They operate quietly in the background of our minds, shaping how we interpret normal relationship friction.

One practical framework I often recommend to clients trying to untangle these automatic reactions is the ENIGMA Core Beliefs Questionnaire. While it sounds clinical, the underlying concept is entirely practical. It gives you a structured way to look past the surface argument and map out the hidden beliefs driving your triggers.

What Are Core Beliefs and Why Do They Hijack Relationships?

Core beliefs are formed early in life as a way to make sense of the world and keep ourselves safe. If you grew up in an environment where affection was unpredictable, you might have formed the core belief: “I am inherently difficult to love,” or “People will always leave when things get tough.”

In the moment, these beliefs don’t feel like thoughts; they feel like absolute facts. When your current partner does something ambiguous—like sighing loudly while washing the dishes—your brain scans your internal library, links the sigh to your core belief, and sounds a false alarm. Suddenly, you aren’t reacting to a tired partner; you are reacting to a decades-old fear of rejection.

The ENIGMA approach helps you pause this automated loop. Instead of asking, “Why is my partner doing this to me?” it prompts you to ask, “What baseline assumption am I bringing into this interaction?”

Step-by-Step: Mapping Your Triggers Using the Questionnaire Approach

To use this framework effectively, you need to turn detective on your own emotional patterns. You can sit down with a notebook and walk through three distinct phases to isolate your core beliefs.

  • Log the Trigger Event: Write down a recent moment where you felt an intense emotional spike (anger, panic, numbness) during a conversation with your partner. Stick to the plain facts. What was said? What time was it? What was the physical setting?
  • Identify the Catastrophic Meaning: Ask yourself what your brain immediately assumed the event meant about the relationship. If your partner rolled their eyes, did your brain translate that to “They don’t respect me,” or “This relationship is failing”?
  • Trace the Belief to Its Origin: Look at the statement you just wrote down. Ask yourself: When was the first time I remember believing this about myself or the world? How has this specific belief influenced my past arguments?

Let’s look at a concrete example. Sarah kept picking fights with her husband, Mark, whenever he wanted to spend an evening playing video games with his friends. On the surface, she argued about fairness and division of labor. But when she used a questionnaire framework to slow down, she realized her core belief was: “If my partner enjoys time away from me, I am disposable.” Once she named that specific belief, she realized Mark’s gaming night had nothing to do with her worth, which allowed her to ask for connection without attacking him.

Shifting From Reaction to Partnership

Uncovering these beliefs is not about blaming yourself for having them. Everyone carries emotional baggage from their past. The goal of examining your core beliefs is to change the job description of your triggers. Instead of letting them dictate your behavior—causing you to lash out, withdraw, or play detective—you start seeing them as outdated warning systems.

When you feel that familiar spike of anxiety or defensiveness rising in your chest, you can literally name the belief out loud to your partner. Saying, “I’m noticing my old belief that you’re going to pull away is taking over right now, even though I know you’re just tired,” changes the dynamic instantly. It moves you from two opponents fighting over who is right to a team looking at an old ghost together.

Taking the time to investigate your underlying assumptions requires patience, but it pays off the next time a routine disagreement threatens to derail your evening. As you start recognizing the patterns behind your emotional spikes, you might find you want a bit more structured guidance to help integrate these insights into your daily communication and long-term relationship habits.
